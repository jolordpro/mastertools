Hair, separation and kicker lights do the same job in different places: they separate the subject from the background; on a dark background the same light could be aimed at the background [10:41][11:14]. He sets this light to about the same as the key, a bit more or less; here 0.4 stop above, which gave a very slight glow on the hair that made a big difference [11:44][12:14][12:47]. Rule of three: the key defines the setup, the fill (or a subtraction panel) controls the shadow side, and the separation light lifts the subject off the background; any 1- to 6-light setup fits it [11:44][14:53]. His two-light example used a subtraction panel opposite the key, to deepen shadows so a strip kicker on the jawline and collarbone stands out [13:51][14:22]. The key is placed about 45 degrees from the camera, raised and angled down, with a big soft box (about 2 ft) recommended for beginners [02:21][02:53][03:26]. Differs from lighting-hair-light-and-background-about-one-stop-under-key, which puts the hair light about one stop under the key. A taste difference, not an error: Wallace works from about -1 to +0.5 stop around the key.

**Use it:** treat rim/hair level as a variable to test around key level, not a fixed under-key figure.
