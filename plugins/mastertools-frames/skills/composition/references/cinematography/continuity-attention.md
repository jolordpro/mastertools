# Continuity and attention

## Quick answer
- Cuts feel invisible when the viewer's attention is already where the next shot's interest appears. `[E-strong]`
- Viewers don't need a coherent 3D map of the scene; attention cues carry continuity. `[E-strong]`
- Tight directorial control synchronizes viewers' eyes and brains. That is a choice, not a quality score. `[E-strong]`

## Continuity rules `[Practice]`
- 180° rule: keep the camera on one side of the action axis so screen direction holds.
- 30° rule: change angle at least about 30° between shots of the same subject, or the cut reads as a jump.
- Match on action: cut mid-movement.
- Eyeline match: a look leads to what the next shot shows.
- Matched exits and entrances: exit frame right, enter frame left.
- Shot / reverse shot with consistent look room.
- Cut on motion or sound to hide the edit.

## Evidence
- Smith 2012, Attentional Theory of Cinematic Continuity `[E-strong]`
  - Explains match-action, matched exits and entrances, shot/reverse-shot, the 180° rule and POV editing through attention.
  - Continuity is perceived without needing a coherent representation of the depicted space.
  - Editors exploit off-screen sounds, conversational turns, motion, gaze and pointing to steer attention.
- Mital et al. 2011; Smith & Mital 2013: gaze clusters across viewers in edited video, peaks after cuts, resets toward center. `[E-strong]`
- Magliano & Zacks 2011: continuity edits within an event are less disruptive than cuts across events (event segmentation). `[E-mixed]` `[Unverified: one lab's result; replication not checked]`
- Hasson et al. 2008, share of cortex with significant inter-subject correlation `[E-strong]`
  - Hitchcock, *Bang! You're Dead*: over 65%.
  - *The Good, the Bad and the Ugly*: 45%.
  - *Curb Your Enthusiasm*: 18%.
  - Unedited park footage: under 5%.
  - SAYS: greater correlation doesn't mean the movie is better.

## Techniques
- `[Infer]` Place the next shot's focal point near where the eye is at the end of the outgoing shot.
- `[Practice]` Use motion to carry the eye across a cut.
- `[Practice]` For graphic transitions, match the position of the outgoing focal point to the incoming one (match cut on shape or color).
- `[Practice]` Break continuity on purpose for disorientation, energy or style (jump cuts, axis crossings; see `rule-breaking`).

## By medium
- Sports broadcast: keep camera on one side of the field for the main game camera so attack direction stays consistent; reverse-angle replays are labeled.
- Motion graphics: transitions are cuts; the eye-trace rule applies.
- Social edits: fast cutting relies on centered focal points because gaze resets toward center after cuts.

## How to measure
- Cut detection; saliency peak position at last frame of shot A vs first frame of shot B (distance).
- Motion direction continuity across cuts.
- Eye-tracking or predicted gaze dispersion over time.

## Failure modes
- Focal point jumps across the frame at every cut, making viewers hunt.
- Accidental axis crossing flipping screen direction.
- Jump cuts that look like errors.

## Critique questions
- Where is the eye at the cut, and where does the next shot need it?
- Is screen direction consistent with the story's geography?
