# Texture and detail

## Quick answer
- Detail attracts attention, so concentrate detail and sharpness at the focal point. `[Practice, consistent with saliency evidence]`
- Raking side light reveals texture; frontal light flattens it. `[Practice]`
- Detail hierarchy matters: painters "finish" the focal area and leave the rest suggested. `[Practice]`

## Definition
- **Texture**: surface quality, actual (photographed) or implied (rendered).
- **Detail**: density of fine information.
- **Sharpness / focus**: edge acuity; blur reduces detail.
- **Grain / noise**: random fine texture from film or sensor.

## Techniques
- `[Practice]` Selective focus to isolate a subject from a busy background.
- `[Practice]` Side light at low angle to sculpt texture (skin, stone, fabric, turf).
- `[Practice]` Lost and found edges: crisp edges at the focal point, soft elsewhere (painting).
- `[Practice]` Texture contrast: smooth subject against rough ground or the reverse.
- `[Practice]` Grain and texture overlays in motion design add tactility; keep them below the legibility threshold for text.

## By medium
- Photography: aperture, shutter (motion blur), focus point.
- Film: focus pulls move attention; shallow focus isolates.
- 3D and motion: depth of field and motion blur cost render time but guide the eye (the Redshift skill covers render tuning).
- Design: texture backgrounds must not compete with text.

## How to measure
- Local sharpness map (Laplacian variance per tile).
- Detail density (edge density per tile).
- Compare focal area sharpness vs background sharpness.
- Noise estimate on flat areas.

## Failure modes
- Sharpest area is not the subject (focus missed).
- Busy texture behind small type.
- Uniform detail everywhere: no hierarchy.

## Critique questions
- Where is the sharpest, most detailed area, and is that the subject?
- Does texture help the mood or only add noise?
