# Master designers

## Quick answer
- Shared principle: reduce to one dominant idea, then execute it with strict control of shape, type and space. `[Practice]`
- Motion title designers (Bass, Cooper, Ferro) translate print composition into time.

## Case studies
### Saul Bass (1920 to 1996)
- Reduction to a single bold symbol; cut-paper shapes; strong figure-ground; title sequences for Hitchcock and Preminger (*Vertigo*, *Psycho*, *Anatomy of a Murder*). `[Practice]`
- Lesson: one image, one idea; motion reveals the idea step by step.

### Paul Rand (1914 to 1996)
- Playful geometric simplicity in logos (IBM, ABC, UPS); wit through shape. `[Practice]`
- Lesson: simple shapes, memorable at any size.

### Josef Müller-Brockmann (1914 to 1996)
- Swiss posters built on strict grids, asymmetric balance, sans-serif type, photography and geometry. `[Practice]`
- Lesson: order and clarity through grids (see `grids-layout`).

### Massimo Vignelli (1931 to 2014)
- Few typefaces, strict grids, NYC subway map and signage system (with Bob Noorda). `[Practice]`
- Lesson: systems over one-offs.

### Paula Scher (1948 to present)
- Expressive large-scale typography as image (Public Theater identity). `[Practice]`
- Lesson: type can be the focal image when scale and rhythm are controlled.

### Kyle Cooper (1962 to present)
- *Se7en* (1995) title sequence: hand-made textures, jittery type, fragmented editing that set a genre tone. `[Practice]`
- Lesson: texture and edit rhythm communicate mood before story.

### Pablo Ferro (1935 to 2018)
- Hand-lettered titles and rapid montage (*Dr. Strangelove* titles). `[Practice] [Unverified]`

## Patterns `[Infer]`
- One dominant idea per piece.
- Constraint (limited palette, few typefaces, strict grid) creates recognizability.
- Figure-ground and shape wit carry meaning without words.
- In motion: reveal order is hierarchy.
