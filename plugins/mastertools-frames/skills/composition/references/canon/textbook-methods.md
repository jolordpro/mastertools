# Textbook methods and worked examples

## Quick answer
- These books teach methods you can run on a frame, not only principles. Twelve are boiled down to step lists in "Methods as procedures".
- Two sources were read at length: Dow (the 1914 ninth edition on Project Gutenberg) and Block (the publisher's free Chapter 9, third edition). Everything else is secondary: publisher pages, tables of contents, reviews, the authors' own blogs. Each entry says which.
- All method tags are `[Practice]`. `RETRO` marks analysis of finished work by a teacher. None of these methods was tested in a controlled study that I found.
- Use the procedures to describe and to propose changes. Never deduct points because a frame lacks a textbook form (see `myths-evidence`).

## How to read an entry
- **Source depth**: READ (book text read) or SECONDARY (pages about the book).
- **Method**: steps in plain words, paraphrased.
- **Worked examples**: named, with what each shows.
- **Best for**: photo, 3D frame, graphics, film.
- **Claude applies**: which procedure below.

- Web search budget ran out partway through the pass. Freeman, Loomis, Mercado, Mateu-Mestre and Roberts rest on tables of contents and reviews. Their step lists below are thin or built by this library (`[Infer]`).

## Bang, Picture This (1991; revised 2016)
- **Source depth**: SECONDARY (author's page, Kirkus review, a classroom handout, a study guide).
- **Method**
  1. Pick a story and one strong emotion. Bang starts from Little Red Riding Hood and a scary picture, because strong feelings are easier to show than mild ones.
  2. Build the picture from flat cut-paper shapes in three colors, then four. No detail, no realism.
  3. Give each character one simple shape and color. The heroine is a red triangle. A review says the mother is a rounder, flat-bottomed blob in violet. `[Unverified: from one search snippet, page not readable]`
  4. Change one thing at a time: size, shape, color, position, space.
  5. After each change, ask what made the picture scarier or calmer, and how the pieces relate to each other.
  6. Write a short note on what you did and why. The classroom version also asks for tape-together arrangements in construction paper.
- **Worked examples**
  - The Red Riding Hood scene: shows that structure alone (shape, size, color, placement) carries fear.
  - The 2016 edition adds an analysis of four illustrations from Bang's picture book *When Sophie Gets Angry* (full title in the book), and ends with a *Dawn* image for the reader to analyze.
- **The principles list**: 10 in the older edition (per the handout), 12 in the 2016 edition (per Kirkus). The library already holds them in `shape`.
- **Best for**: graphics, 3D brand frames (set shapes before detail), key art. Weaker for photo.
- **Evidence**: `[Practice]`. Bang built it in classrooms (children, students, teachers). RETRO does not apply.
- **Claude applies**: Procedure 1.

## Block, The Visual Story (2001; third edition 2021)
- **Source depth**: READ (publisher's free Chapter 9, "Story and Visual Structures") plus the contents page. Other chapters: SECONDARY.
- **Method (from Chapter 9)**
  1. Split the story into exposition, conflict, climax, resolution.
  2. List the story as numbered sequences, each tagged EX, CO, CX or R. Block calls this a Story Sequence List.
  3. Draw the story graph: time across, conflict intensity 0 to 100 up. Generic shape: starts at zero, rises, peaks at the climax, drops in the resolution. Real stories get their own shape (an early spike, saw-tooth rises, and so on).
  4. Write a visual exposition: the rules for how space, line, shape, tone, color, movement and rhythm will be used, taught early so later breaks mean something.
  5. For each component, draw a second graph under the story graph and choose one of three plans: a constant (never changes), a progression (changes steadily), or contrast and affinity (spikes against a baseline).
  6. Law used: more contrast in a component means more visual intensity; more affinity means less.
  7. Put the most contrast on the climax. Return to affinity in the resolution.
  8. Graphs also work on a finished edit. Block says graphing edited work can show where the visuals fail to support the story.
- **Worked examples (all RETRO, Block's own analyses)**
  - *The Meeting* (Block's invented 30-minute story): three attempts to meet. Space plan A: flat to deep progression. Plan B: flat baseline with a deep spike at the end of each attempt, and the third attempt alternates deep and flat shot to shot.
  - *Ninotchka* (1939): the whole film is flat space except one deep shot, Garbo's entrance. Contrast gives her a unique entrance.
  - *The Shining*: mostly deep space, red hues, squares and triangles. Room 237 is flat, green, circular. The contrast foreshadows the conflict. Space also progresses from minor depth to extreme depth while red goes from desaturated pink to saturated red.
  - *Klute*: flat space throughout, used as a constant to mean trapped. Block notes any space can mean trapped if the exposition teaches it (the Shining uses deep space for the same idea).
  - *Jaws*: the opening teaches the audience that the underwater camera plus the music means the shark, so later the shark need not appear.
  - *Matchpoint*: vertical line motif early, diagonals later as stress rises. *Collateral*: the resolution drops from a fast, intense rhythm to a slow wide shot.
  - *North by Northwest* and *Get Out*: full sequence lists and graphs. In the crop duster sequence, intensity stays at zero for the first five minutes and rises with each plane attack.
  - Example 3 (invented coming-of-age story): a five-graph color plan with warm hues constant and blue added at the climax. Block says to make a color script when a graph only says "warm" and "cool".
  - Example 5: two hostile characters, one warm, one cool. Hue contrast shrinks over the story until both are warm. Affinity can express resolution of a conflict, so peaks of contrast do not always sit on peaks of story.
- **Secondary notes on components** (a book-notes blog, not the book): line intensity runs diagonal most, vertical, then horizontal; the brightest object draws the eye first; two moving elements are more intense than still ones.
- **Best for**: broadcast opens, promos, title sequences, series looks, film. Also a single frame (contrast and affinity inside the frame).
- **Evidence**: `[Practice]`. Widely taught (Block's seminars are listed at studios and schools). I found no controlled test of the intensity mapping.
- **Claude applies**: Procedures 2 and 3. Library summary lives in `visual-structure`.

## Dow, Composition (1899; read the 1914 ninth edition)
- **Source depth**: READ (Project Gutenberg #45410, ninth edition, revised and enlarged, 1914). Wording may differ from the 1899 first edition.
- **Method**
  1. Think in line first, then notan (arrangement of dark and light masses), then color. Design before drawing.
  2. Spacing: divide a square or rectangle with lines by eye, with no measuring tool. Make many arrangements, compare, keep the best. Never erase an ink line; start over.
  3. Five principles, all resting on good spacing: Opposition (two lines meet), Transition (a connecting line or curve softens the meeting), Subordination (parts tied to one ruling element by axis, radiation or size contrast), Repetition (a rhythm, not a plain row), Symmetry (equal parts in balance, easy to make commonplace). Dow says these are not recipes.
  4. Notan of line: take a line pattern, thicken some lines, make several versions, lay them side by side, keep the one where every line earns its place.
  5. Two values: start from a well-spaced line design, then add flat black to chosen spaces. Dow warns against aimless blackening: the goal is to learn to tell good arrangements from ugly ones.
  6. Landscape in two values: compose inside a border, trace it several times, darken different spaces, compare, choose. Try the same landscape in different border proportions.
  7. Study masters in two values by blotting in only their dark masses to find the notan scheme underneath.
  8. Three values: add a middle gray, mix three flat washes, test the intervals on a scale, apply to simple designs in several arrangements, then translate masterpieces into three values.
  9. More values: build a scale of white, three grays and black, later seven. Compose freely only after the scale feels easy. In ink painting Dow puts the darks in last, without sharp edges.
- **Worked examples**
  - Corot, Daubigny, Hokusai: used in the master-study exercise (step 7). The point: flat tone relations, not modeling, form the structure.
  - Japanese design books and ink painters (Sesshu, Mokkei, Shubun): pages split into equal sections with different designs; copy enlarged, then make original ones.
  - Whistler for pure line beauty; Rembrandt for tone.
- **Best for**: graphics and layout first (flat shapes), then 3D frames and stills as a structure check. Also film frames as a squint test.
- **Evidence**: `[Practice]`. The master-study step is `RETRO`. Dow himself rejects fixed ratios (see "Where textbooks disagree").
- **Claude applies**: Procedure 7. The library cites Dow 1899 as `[Unverified]` in `value-contrast`; the notan steps above are now checked against the 1914 text.

## Payne, Composition of Outdoor Painting (1941)
- **Source depth**: SECONDARY (course outline, three art blogs, two reviews). The book was not read.
- **Method**
  1. Reduce the scene to its simplest structure. One reviewer says Payne calls this a "mechanical stem" (also described as an "iron pipe structure").
  2. Choose a stem and sketch quick thumbnail variants on it. One blog says Payne used thumbnail notans, black-and-white sketches that test a stem before painting.
  3. Make masses and lines serve the stem. Keep the arrangement natural; Payne wanted forms felt, not spelled out (per one blog).
- **Forms named in the sources** (combine and hide them; definitions are from blogs, not the book)
  - Steelyard: one big mass balanced by a smaller mass placed farther out, like a scale. Put the area of interest near the pivot or on the big mass and link the two.
  - Suspended steelyard: masses high, simple foreground, linked by arcs.
  - L or rectangle: a dominant vertical and horizontal frame the focus.
  - S or compound curve: a winding road, river or shoreline sets rhythm. Build it from lines and edges.
  - O or circle: masses and edges enclose an opening and the eye loops inside it.
  - Triangle or pyramid, cross, diagonal, tunnel, group mass, three spot, radiating lines, balance scale, silhouette, pattern, points of interest.
  - Radiating line: lines converge on the focus. A blog says unbroken lines make the eye move too fast, so break or interrupt them (an untested claim). A painter's article describes using it as one-point-perspective pull and adjusting a scene (adding tire tracks) so the lines point inward.
- **Worked examples**: Payne's book uses thumbnail diagrams of famous paintings by many artists (RETRO). No specific painting titles were named in any source I read. A painter's article gives two of his own: *All Bundled Up* (hay bales lead back to buildings) and *Roasted Reds* (tracks added to strengthen a weak road).
- **Best for**: wide landscapes, establishing shots, 3D environment frames, photo layout planning.
- **Evidence**: `[Practice]` for planning. No tests that a stem improves a picture. Use as description only, never a score (as `geometry-proportion` already says).
- **Claude applies**: Procedure 5.

## Gurney, Color and Light (2010) and Imaginative Realism (2009)
- **Source depth**: SECONDARY for the books. READ for the author's blog posts "How Many Values?" (2018-01-10), "Shapewelding", "Linking Darks" and "Notan". The books' own chapters were not read, and I did not find the phrase "shapes of light" in any source, so I do not use it.
- **Method (from the blog)**
  1. Sort values into two families: a light family and a dark family. Each family may vary inside itself. A gap sits between them.
  2. Fewer tones is better, he says, citing Howard Pyle. Practice with two or three tones.
  3. Link like to like: join dark shapes into one dark mass, light shapes into one light mass ("shapewelding", "linking darks"). Keep linked darks nearly flat.
  4. Let unimportant edges disappear. The eye finishes lost contours. Define a white by a dark behind it, not by an outline.
  5. Save the strongest light-dark contrast for the center of interest.
  6. Arrangement matters as much as the number of values.
  7. Notan planning: block in the big near-white and near-black masses with a flat brush first, add detail inside each family after, keep middle tones out. Practice: a one-minute notan with one dark and a big brush.
- **Worked examples (RETRO, Gurney's analyses)**
  - Pyle, *Travels of the Soul*: lights grouped into one mass, darks into another, gradation between them.
  - Pyle, *Mermaid*: neighboring tones fuse into larger abstract shapes even with many figures.
  - Stanhope Forbes, *The Seine Boat*: dark garments and net kept flat so they frame the light shapes.
  - Edward Simmons, *Old Woman Peeling an Orange*: the shawl links to the door and wall, which gives the head and hands more emphasis.
  - Sargent, *Padre Sebastiano*: hat links to coat, papers to sheets, and a concentrated oval of light on the face.
  - Dean Cornwell moonlight scene: the "whites" are a family of mid cool tones; the highest values are saved for small lit windows.
  - Comic masters (Crane, Caniff, Foster): story told in black and white only.
- **Best for**: 3D frames and stills (value grouping before color), key art, concept frames. Strong for lighting decisions in C4D because it is about large light and dark shapes.
- **Evidence**: `[Practice]`.
- **Claude applies**: Procedure 9, and the notan step of Procedure 7.

## Bacher, Dream Worlds (2007) and Vision (2018)
- **Source depth**: SECONDARY. *Dream Worlds* chapter list is confirmed (Parka Blogs); its chapter text was not read. The rules below come from Bacher's *Mulan* style guide as reported by Animation Obsessive. I could not confirm these rules appear in the book.
- **Method (Mulan style guide, 1995 list; a seventh rule added 1997)**
  1. Balance busy areas against quiet areas.
  2. Balance straight shapes against curved shapes.
  3. Balance positive space against negative space.
  4. Keep silhouettes clearly readable.
  5. Leave an empty stage for the action. He compared this to the empty settings in *Bambi*.
  6. Do not overload the picture with information. If detail is needed, change lighting, focus or lens.
  7. Rhythmic composition (added 1997).
  - Extra guidelines: do not repeat character shapes in the background; avoid parallel lines, equal spacing and straight lines; avoid extreme perspective and build depth through focus levels instead.
- **Worked examples**
  - Mulan final shot: busy sides, smooth ground, hazy forest, curved bamboo, open area for the characters, no repeated shapes (Animation Obsessive's reading).
  - Mulan temple scene: stone tablets simplified and treated like landscape designs; the final landed between Bacher's proposal and the busier original.
  - *Dream Worlds* chapters include Composition, Staging and Formats, Rhythm and Style Variety, and Value and Color. Their content was not verified.
- **Best for**: animation layouts, 3D brand frames, broadcast graphics where the action needs clear stage space.
- **Evidence**: `[Practice]`, documented (`DOC`) as the studio's guide.
- **Claude applies**: Procedure 8.

## Mateu-Mestre, Framed Ink (2010) and Framed Ink 2 (2020)
- **Source depth**: SECONDARY (Goodreads summary and reader reviews, publisher and archive descriptions). No chapter text or contents list found.
- **What is confirmed**
  - Volume 1 moves in three stages: compose a single image, compose a steady shot, then compose for continuity across shots. It applies them to three sample stories: a train accident, a cowboy tale, bikers approaching a mysterious house.
  - Reviewers say the book breaks images into shapes, values and light, with simple black-and-white thumbnails, and praise its staging: camera angle, lens, guiding the eye, order against chaos, scale.
  - Volume 2 covers energy inside the frame: push-pull, tension-relaxation, pressure-release, balance and imbalance, and how horizontal, vertical and square formats change the composition.
- **Method**: no step list was verifiable. Procedure 4 is built by this library from the confirmed pieces plus Gurney and Dow (`[Infer]`). I could not confirm a "value staging" method in the book itself.
- **Worked examples**: the three sample stories above; I found no detail on what each shows.
- **Best for**: storyboards, 3D frames, film frames, social aspect-ratio cutdowns (format energy).
- **Evidence**: `[Practice]` (reviews only).
- **Claude applies**: Procedure 4.

## Roberts, Mastering Composition (2007)
- **Source depth**: SECONDARY. The Library of Congress table of contents is read; reviews give the rest.
- **Method (contents show these steps; details not verified)**
  1. Armatures: study the masters, learn the four most important compositional lines and eight common armatures, horizontals and verticals, the cruciform, "twelve composition basics".
  2. The emphasized-edge sketch.
  3. Think in value masses, not subjects. Seek strong abstract shapes. Use a viewfinder to crop. Change your viewpoint. Make thumbnails. Copy a painting you like. Do a composition a day.
  4. Color shapes: color as shapes of three qualities (hue, value, intensity), temperature, edge control with edge transition scales.
  5. Guide the eye: evaluate the composition as a whole, attune to the role of contrast, lead the eye around the picture plane.
- **Reviewer notes**: armatures are the best section (some old master examples built on mathematical geometry); one reviewer found the eye-movement chapter poorly explained.
- **Worked examples**: five step-by-step painting demonstrations (mostly Roberts's own landscapes and still lifes), including "Plan Big Masses When You Paint" and "Lead the Eye Around the Picture Plane".
- **Best for**: planning stage for paintings and 3D frames; the crop and mass steps work on photos.
- **Evidence**: `[Practice]`.
- **Claude applies**: Procedure 11.

## Loomis, Creative Illustration (1947)
- **Source depth**: SECONDARY, thin. Publisher page and one review.
- **What is confirmed**
  - Seven sections: Line, Tone, Color, Telling the Story, Creating Ideas, Fields of Illustration, Experimenting and Studies.
  - It covers how the eye studies a picture and shows weak compositions with the reason each is weak.
  - Tone: block in the picture in tonal values first, add detail after. Tonal levels are used to strengthen and balance the composition. A reviewer says this makes small thumbnails easy to scale up.
  - Loomis cautions against leaning on a camera, which records too much detail.
- **Method**: the eye-path steps in Procedure 6 are built by this library from "eye studies a picture" and "tone first". I could not verify Loomis's own eye-path method. Do not attribute the steps to him.
- **Worked examples**: deliberately weak compositions (no titles found).
- **Best for**: illustration, key art, 3D frames.
- **Evidence**: `[Practice]`, thin.
- **Claude applies**: Procedure 6 (tone-first block-in).

## Mercado, The Filmmaker's Eye (2010)

Breaking the rules (examples where a break pays off). The book opens with a short section on principles and then a chapter on the image system.

- **Worked examples**: hundreds of film frames with callouts. Specific films were not verifiable. One third-party summary says the book introduces thirds, leading lines and depth of field as basics; I could not confirm the book's own wording.

## Albers, Interaction of Color (1963)
- **Source depth**: SECONDARY (Yale University Press pages, Wikipedia, a museum page, a search snippet of the table of contents).
- **Method**
  1. Learn by experiment before theory. Albers put practice first and presented color systems last in his courses (per Wikipedia).
  2. Put the same color on two different grounds and see it change. Contents list "one color appears as two".
  3. Find two different colors that look alike on different grounds. Contents list "two different colors look alike".
  4. The book also covers intensity and temperature, vibrating and vanishing boundaries, the illusion of transparency, and reversed grounds.
  5. Conclusion for practice: judge a color in its surround, never from a swatch alone.
- **Worked examples**: the studies above. *Homage to the Square* (from 1949): nested squares, usually three or four per painting, built to show color interaction, with the maker's paint colors noted on the back.
- **Best for**: graphics over live video (lower thirds, tickers), brand color checks, grading across cuts.
- **Evidence**: `[Practice]`. Color relativity is also a library claim (`color`) with E-strong support. I checked only that the book makes it.
- **Claude applies**: Procedure 10.

# Methods as procedures

Run these on a frame (F1…Fn, 4×4 grid, columns A to D, rows 1 to 4). Report where, which mechanism, and a rung label where one applies. All are `[Practice]` unless marked `[Infer]`. They describe and propose; they do not score.

## 1. Bang build-up (shape-level emotion check)
Source: Bang. Use for: graphics, brand frames, key art.
1. State the intended feeling in one word (threat, calm, pride, speed).
2. Reduce the frame to 3 or 4 flat shapes in 3 or 4 flat colors. List each: shape, size, position, color, value, direction.
3. Check each against the intent using the `shape` principles (pointed vs round, big vs small, high vs low, centered vs edge).
4. Find the single change that would push the feeling harder (one variable only). Test it as a flat mock.
5. Compare the two flat versions. Keep the one that reads stronger at thumbnail size.
6. Only then add detail back.
Output: the shape that carries the feeling (cell), the one change proposed, rung (FIX if shapes fight the intent, C→S if neutral, S→W if shapes already carry it and the change sharpens it).

## 2. Block contrast and affinity audit (one frame or one cut)
Source: Block. Use for: a still, a keyframe, a cut between two shots.
1. List the seven components: space, line, shape, tone, color, movement, rhythm.
2. For each, mark contrast or affinity inside the frame (or across the cut): for example, flat vs deep, diagonal vs horizontal, warm vs cool.
3. Sum up: where is the highest-contrast component, and where in the frame (cell)?
4. Check that the highest-contrast component sits on the intended focus.
5. Check for tied peaks: if every component is at contrast, the frame has no peak (a failure mode in `visual-structure`).
6. Check that the story moment matches. A calm story beat with high contrast everywhere, or a climax with affinity everywhere, is a mismatch.
Output: component table (contrast, affinity, constant), the peak, and one component to pull back or push.

## 3. Block story graph plan (opens, promos, sequences)
Source: Block Chapter 9 (read). Use for: a broadcast open, promo, title sequence, series look.
1. Write a numbered list of sequences or beats and tag each EX, CO, CX or R.
2. Draw the story graph: time across, intensity 0 to 100 up. Do not force a straight ramp; mark spikes, saw-tooth rises and an opening spike if the piece starts hot.
3. Write the visual exposition: one line per component saying what it means (for example "flat and cool means ordinary; deep and warm means stakes").
4. For each component choose constant, progression or contrast and affinity. Draw it under the story graph.
5. Put the largest contrast spike on the climax. Hold back at least one component until then (Block's *Ninotchka* and *Shining* examples).
6. Resolution returns to affinity.
7. For a finished cut, graph what is on screen and compare to step 2. Mismatches are the notes.
8. Check Example 5 case: if the story is about reconciliation, contrast should fall over time while intensity stays high.
Output: two aligned graphs, the held-back component, and mismatches by timestamp.

## 4. Value plan for a storytelling frame `[Infer]`
Built from Mateu-Mestre's reported emphasis on black-and-white thumbnails of shapes, light and frame energy, plus Dow and Gurney. Use for: storyboard frames, 3D frames, social cutdowns.
1. Make a small thumbnail: black, white, one gray. No color.
2. Place the lightest light and darkest dark next to each other at the story focus (cell).
3. Group the other darks into one or two masses. Keep them flat.
4. Check the hero silhouette reads on its own.
5. Check push-pull in the frame: which edge pushes in, which side gives room (tension against release). Name where the eye rests.
6. Re-run at each delivery format (16:9, 1:1, 9:16). Framed Ink 2 says format changes the composition, so recheck focus, masses and safe areas.
Output: the three-value thumbnail described by cell, focus cell, format notes. Not a score.

## 5. Payne armature check (description only)
Source: Payne via secondary sources. No lore scoring.
1. Sketch or describe the frame as 3 to 5 big masses and lines.
2. Name which stem best describes it: steelyard, suspended steelyard, L, S, O, triangle, cross, diagonal, tunnel, group mass, three spot, radiating line.
3. State what the stem does here: balance (steelyard), eye route (S, tunnel, radiating), enclosure (O), stability (triangle).
4. If lines converge (radiating), check they are broken or interrupted somewhere rather than a clean unbroken arrow (untested claim; offer as a suggestion).
5. Planning use: try two other stems on the same subject as thumbnails and compare.
6. Never write "fails the pyramid" or "should be an S". If no stem fits, say "no single stem, mass plan is mixed" and move on.
Output: one line of description plus what the stem does. No deduction.

## 6. Eye-path trace and outtake compare `[Infer]`
Built from Loomis (tone first, "how the eye studies a picture"), Freeman (eye-path diagram, outtake comparison) and `attention-saliency`.
1. Block in tone first: squint or posterize to 3 values.
2. Mark the strongest contrast junction, the brightest area, faces and gaze, and pointing edges.
3. Trace the likely path in order: entry point (usually the strongest contrast or a face), focus, one or two stops, exit.
4. Mark leaks: where an edge, a gaze or a bright patch pulls the path out of the frame or away from the subject.
5. Mark the end of the path. Does it end on the intended focus?
6. For a set of near-identical frames (burst, renders), repeat on each and name the one element that changed between the best and its neighbors.
7. State this as a hypothesis. People's paths depend on task and content (see `attention-saliency`). If a saliency model is available, compare.
Output: path in grid cells (F1·B3 to F1·C2), leaks, and the single change that fixes the worst leak.

## 7. Dow notan pass
Source: Dow (read). Use for: any frame.
1. Reduce to two values (threshold; for a photo, blur slightly first to drop texture). Keep what remains: only big light and dark shapes.
2. Ask: do the big shapes read as a design, with varied sizes, a clear dominant shape, and no tie between two equal masses?
3. Check each shape earns its place. A shape that serves nothing is clutter; a dominant shape that fights the focus is a problem (Dow, in his line exercise: one line that dominates the rest breaks the harmony).
4. Repeat with three values (add a middle gray). Check the middle value separates the focus from background rather than creating mid-gray soup.
5. Try one or two alternative arrangements (swap which masses are dark). Dow's method is many arrangements, then pick.
6. Optional: five or seven values only after steps 1 to 4 pass.
Output: area share and position of each value mass, whether the two-value version reads, and the one mass to merge, move or flip. Use `metric-recipes` k-means (k=3, k=4) for numbers.
Caution: a deliberately narrow tonal range (low key, high key) is a style choice, not a fail.

## 8. Bacher shape staging
Source: Mulan style guide as reported. Use for: 3D frames, animation layouts, graphic frames with action.
1. Mark busy areas and quiet areas. Are they balanced?
2. Mark straight shapes and curved shapes. Balanced?
3. Mark positive and negative space. Is there an empty stage where the action or text lands?
4. Fill the hero black. Does the silhouette read against the background?
5. Check for repeated shapes between hero and background (same silhouette echoing = visual tangle).
6. Check for parallel lines and even spacing; vary them.
7. Check depth: is it built with focus and haze levels or with extreme perspective? Prefer the first when the frame is crowded.
8. Check rhythm: do details frame the stage in a deliberate pattern?
Output: the busiest region, the empty stage cell, and one shape echo to break.

## 9. Gurney value grouping
Source: Gurney's blog. Use for: lighting and look decisions in 3D, key art, photo grade.
1. Name the light family and the dark family. How many distinct values live in each?
2. Find scattered small darks. Can they link into one or two dark masses?
3. Find isolated lights. Can they join?
4. Is the highest contrast at the focus and weaker elsewhere?
5. Count values. Could the frame read with two or three tones? If not, which values are doing no work?
6. Find outlines that could be lost into a neighbor. Propose merges, not blurs.
Output: the dark mass to build (cells), the values to cut, the highest-contrast junction.

## 10. Albers relativity check
Source: Albers (secondary). Use for: lower thirds, tickers, brand color in context, grading across cuts.
1. Identify the key color and the grounds it will sit on (live video, graphic plates, team colors).
2. View the color on at least two clearly different grounds (light and dark; warm and cool).
3. Note where it shifts in hue, value or apparent size. A color that looks right on one ground and wrong on another is a context problem, not a swatch problem.
4. Check edges between near-equal values for vibration (hue difference with similar lightness).
5. Fix by changing the ground, the edge, or the value, not by chasing the swatch number.
6. For brand-critical colors, test on every ground in the production list.
Output: the ground where the color breaks, the shift observed, the fix.

## 11. Roberts crop and mass check
Source: Roberts (contents only). Use for: photo crops, 3D camera placement, key art crops.
1. Crop with a viewfinder-style frame; try several crops of the same view.
2. In each crop, name the three to five value masses (not the subjects).
3. Pick the crop whose masses form the strongest abstract shapes: clear sizes (big, medium, small), one dominant.
4. Check the edges: where do masses touch or cut the frame? Avoid tangents and accidental joints.
5. Try a different viewpoint before settling (Roberts's list includes it).
6. Make thumbnails of the best two and compare.
Output: the chosen crop in coordinates, the dominant mass, tangents found.

## 12. Mercado shot rule-and-break check
Source: Mercado (structure only). Use for: film, promo frames, 3D camera shots.
1. Name the shot type (close up, over the shoulder, establishing, dolly, and so on).
2. Why it works: what job does this shot type do in a sequence?
3. How it works: which visual rules are visible in the frame (placement, headroom, lead room, depth cues, lens)?
4. Technical: does the lens and camera move suit the job?
5. Breaks: list any rule the frame breaks. Judge each with `rule-breaking`: systematic, nameable meaning, everything else under control.
6. Output a keep, fix or leave verdict for each break.
Output: shot type, rules met, breaks judged.

# Where textbooks disagree with the research

Cross-checked against `myths-evidence`, `geometry-proportion`, `color` and `aesthetic-models`.

1. **Fixed proportions (golden section, root rectangles).**
   - Dow (ninth edition, Chapter V) grants that fixed ratios helped ordinary work, says the finest work came from trained judgment, and says proportion is measured by one's sense of relations, not by any formula. He sides with the research.
   - Tschichold's page canon (per Wikipedia) describes a text block in the golden section. One review of Roberts says some old master examples are built on mathematical geometry. The library marks golden ratio as `[Lore]` for quality (Olariu: 565 paintings averaged 1.34, not 1.618). Treat these as layout conventions and planning aids, never as a pass or fail.
2. **Armatures and stems as quality.**
   - Payne-derived forms and Roberts's armatures are presented as structures behind good pictures. I found no preference test of any stem. `geometry-proportion` keeps them as scaffolds and description only.
3. **Block's intensity mapping.**
   - Contrast equals intensity, affinity equals calm, and peaks should match story peaks. This is a design theory. I found no controlled test that audiences feel intensity this way. The brightest object drawing the eye (a secondary note on Block) agrees with the library's luminance-first finding. The ranking of line orientations (diagonal most intense) is `[Practice]` in the library, not measured.
4. **"Warm advances, cool recedes."**
   - A secondary note on Block says warm colors read as closer and cool as farther. The library marks this `[E-mixed]`; aerial perspective (lost contrast and saturation) is the more reliable depth cue. Use depth cues first, hue second.
5. **Bang's emotional principles.**
   - They came from classroom experiments and her own judgment. Her "center draws most attention" matches the library's center-bias finding. "Upper half freer, lower half heavier," "pale backgrounds feel safer," and "same color links more than same shape" have no test in this library. The library already tags them `[Practice]` with exact wording unverified.
6. **"Fewer values is better" and notan as a test.**
   - Gurney quotes Pyle on this, and Dow builds the course on it. The research supports luminance carrying structure (`value-contrast`). It does not say a two-tone read is a quality threshold. Low-key and high-key work deliberately narrows the range, and film trended darker over decades. Use notan to test structure, not to rank taste.
7. **Albers against color rule-lists.**
   - Albers put experience first and color systems last. Harmony schemes (Itten-style) are taught as rules elsewhere. The library marks harmony schemes `[E-mixed]`. Albers's relativity point is the safer working rule: judge in context.
8. **Eye paths.**
   - Freeman and Loomis teach tracing how the eye moves. Research shows paths depend on task, content and time (`attention-saliency`). A traced path is a hypothesis, not a measurement. Say so in the output.
9. **"Design is the most important factor."**
   - This is the publisher's summary of Freeman's stance. `aesthetic-models` says form drives early response and meaning, context and expertise drive later response. Score form and meaning separately.
10. **Rules as law versus rules to break.**
    - Mercado's structure ("learn the rules, then break them") and Tschichold's later reversal agree with `rule-breaking`. One possible disagreement: a secondary summary says Mercado's introduction lists the rule of thirds among basics. If the book teaches it as a law, the library marks it `[Lore]`. I could not confirm the book's wording.
11. **What "notan" means.**
    - Wikipedia says Dow treated notan as a quality of well-balanced painting, which is not how the word is typically used in Japanese aesthetics. In this library "notan" means a value-mass study, nothing more.
