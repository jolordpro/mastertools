# Interviews and offers

Sections: §1 Loops by employer type · §2 Mock panel protocol · §3 Story bank · §4 Recruiter screen and level pre-wire · §5 Offers and negotiation · §6 Decide mode and freelance pricing

## 1. Loops by employer type

Always ask the recruiter for the format, the interviewers' roles and how the role is leveled. Typical shapes (verify per company):

| Employer type | Typical loop | Usually decides it |
|---|---|---|
| Big tech, product motion | Recruiter screen → hiring manager → portfolio presentation (45–60 min) → craft deep dive → app critique or prototyping exercise → cross-functional (engineer or PM) → leadership/behavioral at Staff+ | Portfolio presentation and the leveling read across rounds |
| AI lab, brand or launch motion | Recruiter → hiring manager → portfolio review → craft or take-home → cross-functional partners → values | Craft, taste and speed; how the person works with writers, producers and marketers |
| Streaming or entertainment (e.g. Netflix) | Recruiter → hiring manager → portfolio to a panel → several one-on-ones with creative, production and partners; culture probed throughout; references taken seriously | The panel, and culture fit stated in the company's own terms |
| Studio or agency | Reel → creative director conversation → test or trial project, sometimes paid | The reel and the CD's read of taste |

Many postings list their own culture or values lines. Map stories to whatever lines the target posting names.

## 2. Mock panel protocol

Run it when a loop is scheduled.
1. Get the material: the person's 2–3 cases (text, links or files) and the req's requirement lines.
2. The person "presents" (pasted outline or narration). Keep each case to problem → constraints → own role → decisions and trade-offs → outcome → what they'd change.
3. Play three interviewers in turn and ask what each would ask:
   - **Design lead:** "Why this timing? What did you try first? What would you cut?"
   - **Cross-functional partner** (engineer, producer or marketer): "How did this get built or delivered? What broke? What did you hand off?"
   - **Bar-raiser:** "What did *you* do versus the team? What's the measurable result? What would you do differently?"
4. Score each case 1–5 on the req's top requirement lines and on the target level's scope. Senior reads as end-to-end ownership of complex work; Staff reads as direction and systems others adopted.
5. Return the three changes that would move the score most, and rewritten opening lines for each case.

Flag what panels catch: unattributed team outcomes, metrics that don't add up, "we" with no "I", no trade-offs, a reel with no reasoning, ignoring reduced motion or performance in product work.

## 3. Story bank

Prepare one 90-second story each, with a result:
- Set direction on a loose brief
- Gave and received hard feedback; disagreed with a creative director or lead
- Acted without being asked
- A failure and what changed after
- A standard, toolkit or system others adopted (Staff: required)
- A deadline-pressure call (live, launch, client in the room)

Shape: situation in one line, the decision, what the person did, the result with a number, what they learned.

## 4. Recruiter screen and level pre-wire

- **Level.** "How is this role leveled, and what does the next level look like here?" Then state the level the evidence supports, in one sentence of scope.
- **Range.** "What's the range for this role and level?" Ask before giving a number.
- **Expectations.** Once level, band, bonus and equity structure are known, give a range anchored at or above the posted band's upper half when the evidence supports it, framed as total comp: "For this scope I'm targeting total comp in the {{X}}–{{Y}} range, depending on the full package."
- **Current pay.** Don't volunteer it, and don't give a number that isn't true (rounding up is a misstatement). Many US states and cities restrict salary-history questions. Read the person's state law in the official text first: what the employer may ask, whether the person can request the pay scale, and what changes if the person volunteers a number. If asked anyway: "I'd rather focus on the value of this role. Once I understand the level and how equity works here, I'll give you a clear number."
- **Hold the number.** Give expectations only once level, band, bonus and equity structure are known. Until then: "Once I understand the level and how equity works here, I'll give you a clear number."
- **Competing processes.** True and specific is fine ("I'm in final rounds elsewhere at Staff"). Never invent one.

## 5. Offers and negotiation

**Normalize each offer** to annual total comp: base + target bonus + equity per year + sign-on in year 1 only. Show year 1 and steady state separately, and when each piece becomes spendable (cliffs, vesting cadence, front- or back-loading).

**Equity.**
- Public RSUs: ~100% of face value, with a sensitivity line (a 30% stock move changes the grant 30%).
- Private options: value on the spread, (latest preferred price − strike) × shares, never on a recruiter's "paper value". Discount by stage, all rules of thumb: late-stage with tenders or secondaries ~50–70% of spread; Series B–D ~25–50%; seed or Series A plan on zero. If strike is unknown, ask; 409A common prices have often landed around 30–40% of the last preferred price (old data; label it).
- Ask for: share count and percent of fully diluted shares, strike, latest preferred, 409A date, liquidation preferences, ISO or NSO, post-termination exercise window, early exercise, tender history, refresh policy.

**Cross-check the offer against the level's structure.** A component that doesn't match the level (a Staff-scope job description with a Senior bonus target or band) often means the offer is pegged a level low. That finding is usually worth more than any base negotiation.

**Sequence.**
1. Get the missing facts.
2. Negotiate level first, quoting the job's own scope language against the ladder.
3. Then the package: when base is capped, push equity and sign-on.
4. Use the most liquid competing number to raise the other offer.
5. Exploding deadline: ask for an extension tied to a real date ("I'll have a decision from my other final round by {{date}}; can we hold until then?"), and ask the slower company whether it can accelerate.

**Scripts** (adapt; keep them short and true):
```text
Level: "Thanks, I'm excited about this. The role as described ('{{scope quote}}') matches your L{{n+1}} definition ('{{ladder quote}}'). Before we talk numbers, can we look at leveling this at L{{n+1}}?"
Extension: "I want to give you a clear yes. I'm in a final round that concludes by {{date}}. Could we extend the deadline to {{date}}?"
Package: "If base is fixed at {{X}}, I'd need the equity at {{Y}} and a sign-on of {{Z}} to make this work."
Accelerate (to the slower company): "I have an offer with a deadline of {{date}}. You're my first choice; is there any way to reach a decision by then?"
```

For tax or exercise decisions on equity, suggest a tax professional; this is information, not tax or legal advice.

## 6. Decide mode and freelance pricing

**Expected comp.** EV = p × (pay over the horizon if the move works, counting the months before it starts) + (1 − p) × (pay on the fallback path). Example: a 60% chance of a $130K-base job by month 6, against staying freelance: 0.6 × $436K + 0.4 × $281K ≈ $375K over 3 years. Show the math in 2–4 lines and let the person swap in their own odds. Rank by cumulative expected pay; also report the pay rate each path reaches by the end of the horizon and its long-run ceiling. If the person states a target ("$300K by year 3"), give the odds of hitting it and say when the cumulative winner and the target winner differ.

**Comparable pay.**
- Job: base + target bonus + risk-adjusted equity per year + benefits at ~20% of base (BLS's ~30% benefits share includes paid leave, which salary already covers; use ~30% when the person needs family health coverage).
- Freelance: gross − business costs (ask; ~$8K a year is a placeholder) − ~7% of net for the employer half of Social Security and Medicare.

**Constraints.** Rank within stated constraints (location, schedule, family). When a constraint caps pay, estimate what relaxing it would be worth and let the person decide with the number.

**Freelance pricing.**
- Compute current comparable income, then a break-even table: freelance gross (rate × booked days) needed to match each realistic in-house offer. Say whether that rate × days is realistic.
- Diagnose the binding constraint: low booked days usually matter more than rate. If they turned work down, rate is the lever; if not, demand is.
- Levers, roughly by impact: niche positioning, selling direction rather than execution, fixed-fee productized offers, retainers, studio margin on a small team. Raising rates for new clients is a no-regret move, not a strategy.
