# Presenting work and handling feedback

Use when delivering options with a rationale, or when the user pushes back, asks for revisions or wants to test copy. Page numbers are Sullivan, *Hey Whipple, Squeeze This*, 3rd ed. (2008), mostly chapter 11. His chapter is about humans presenting to clients; the rules below translate it for an AI writer and its user.

Contents: 1. Writing the rationale · 2. Choosing what to show · 3. Taking feedback · 4. Protecting the idea · 5. Testing and research

## 1. Writing the rationale

- **Argue from strategy, not taste.** The "Why" line is a chain: the audience fact, the single proposition, why this execution carries it. Never "punchy", "fun", "I love it". Think like a creative, talk like an accountant (pp.242-244).
- **Say what it makes the reader feel and why that serves the goal.** Most buying is emotional with reasons added after; a rationale that only gives logic misses why the line works (pp.203, 244).
- **Don't oversell.** No "you'll love this", and don't call your own work "bold" or "risky" - "risk" is the word decision-makers fear. If it breaks a convention, say it goes against the grain of the category (p.241).
- **Pre-answer the likely objections** (too similar to competitors, too negative, too indirect) and never raise a question you can't answer (p.241).
- **If the piece needs a footnote to make sense, rewrite the piece.** Keep the explanation of how you got there short and ready (p.288).
- **For a script or concept, tell it in two or three sentences first,** then show the full text. Showing everything at once makes readers skim ahead (pp.240-241).
- **Show copy in place when possible** - the headline on the page, the subject line in an inbox preview, the line on the poster in the street (pp.241-242).

## 2. Choosing what to show

- **Every alternate must be shippable.** Never include an option you wouldn't want chosen; filler gets picked, and people judge a set by its weakest member (pp.242, 282).
- **Include one stretch option.** Don't leave the boldest idea out because "they'd never buy it". Go as far as you can and let the reader pull it back (p.242).
- **Show range when giving options:** vary the angle and the emotion (funny, serious, warm), not just the wording (p.279).
- **Several requested messages means several pieces.** When asked to fit three benefits into one piece, propose three pieces first; if the user insists, ask which audience they're willing not to reach (pp.253-254).

## 3. Taking feedback

- **Treat feedback as information.** Restate the concern back ("you want X clearer and the tone less formal, right?"). Don't defend in the same breath (pp.244-245).
- **Start from their position and build a bridge back to yours.** Go in assuming a real chance you're wrong; it makes the argument more convincing (pp.242-244).
- **When feedback is vague** ("I don't love it", "I'll know it when I see it"), don't just produce more variations. Ask what specifically feels wrong - the words, the tone, the claim, the length, who it sounds like - and what a right answer would do. More options without a criterion don't help (pp.210-212).
- **When several reviewers disagree,** look for agreement. If most name the same weak piece, fix it. Don't try to satisfy every note; that waters the work down. A cheap technique: ask each person which single piece is weakest (pp.288-289).

## 4. Protecting the idea

- **Concede details, protect the idea.** Wording, length and phrasing can flex; the core idea is what to defend. Winning every small argument can cost the idea (pp.245-247).
- **Watch for death by a thousand edits.** When accumulated changes have drained the idea, say so plainly and offer to restart from the strategy rather than patching (p.253).
- **After a rejection, come back with something at least as strong,** using what the rejection taught you. Keep killed ideas; they can return later (pp.266-268).
- **After approval, check the final version** (line breaks, punctuation, the mock-up) - approved copy often gets damaged on the way out (p.266).

## 5. Testing and research

- **Research decides what to say; craft decides how to say it.** The best use of research is up front: listening to how customers talk about the category (p.248).
- **Concept testing misleads** when it shows rough versions (no tone), asks whether people "like" it, or asks them to critique like experts. If the user wants to test copy: use finished or realistic versions, ask what people took away, and compare options against a real goal (pp.248-249).
- **Polling features produces an average execution** - use customer input for strategy, not to design the ad (pp.251-253).
- **The rubric is a quality filter, not a prediction.** Never tell a user a line "would test well" or that an audience would prefer it. Say it's craft judgment and what a real test should check.
- Note: Sullivan's research chapter is practitioner opinion with anecdotes, not measured evidence. For evidence, see 10.
