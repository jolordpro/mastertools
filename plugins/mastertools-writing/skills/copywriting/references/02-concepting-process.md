# Concepting process (agency model)

Contents: 1. Before writing · 2. Workflow · 3. Idea starters · 4. Generating in volume · 5. Judging ideas · 6. Campaigns and platforms · 7. Social-era updates

Sullivan page numbers refer to *Hey Whipple, Squeeze This*, 3rd edition (2008).

## 1. Before writing

- **Do the background work.** Read the brand's positioning and the competitors' (which adjectives are taken?), the client's past advertising, what customers say online, and the media the work will run in. Then try to feel the reader's day, not just read about it (Sullivan pp.29-35).
- **Learn the category's clichés** before writing, so you can avoid them: every category has its stock pictures and phrases (Sullivan pp.35, 55).
- **Demand a tight, simple strategy.** It should fit in one sentence, like a film logline, and describe a shift: move people who feel X to feel Y. A vague brief inhibits; an over-specified one is a disguised execution (Sullivan pp.31-33).
- **Check that the message matters to someone.** Test the *what* (plain benefit statements) before the *how* (Sullivan pp.33-34).
- **When the client uses a loaded word** ("clean", "bold", "premium", "authentic"), ask what it means to them and for an example. Same word, different pictures; it causes most revision rounds (Futur creative brief questionnaire).

## 2. Workflow

1. **Question the brief.** Restate it as a question, then re-ask it from several angles; a question you don't already know the answer to stretches the thinking (Sullivan p.39). Check it is really a communication problem (Trott: think upstream).
2. **One proposition.** One sentence the audience should believe. If the brief has three, push back - three messages means three pieces.
3. **Dig for the truth.** What is the truest thing about the product, including what is unflattering? Find the human truth of the whole category, not just the product: hair colour is about self-esteem; flowers are often an apology (Sullivan pp.40-41).
4. **Find the tension.** A villain, a convention, a contradiction: a competitor, a pain, or a rival from another category entirely (Sullivan pp.47-48). Later editions phrase it as truth + conflict = platform.
5. **Narrow the audience.** One person. What do "people like us" do (Godin)? In which moments should the brand come to mind?
6. **Pick the mood before you write** (thoughtful, angry, stark) so tone matches product and reader (Sullivan p.43).
7. **Say it plainly first, then say it well.** One device: start a line with "this is an ad about..." and keep writing, then cut the opener. At worst you've focused the point (Sullivan p.42). Then work the strategy sentence like clay - shorter, in slang, punched up - until it stops sounding like a sales conference (Sullivan pp.42-43).
8. **Generate in volume** (section 4).
9. **Work words and pictures together.** The line should not repeat the image; together they make 1 + 1 = 3 (Trott; Sullivan pp.92-94).
10. **Write the whole piece before deciding an idea fails.** Writing it out often fixes what bothered you (Sullivan p.163).
11. **Build platforms, not one-offs** (section 6).
12. **Write hot, edit cold** (Sullivan p.83). Keep only A-plus work; shelve the A-minus for later rather than deleting it.
13. **Judge against section 5**, then present a few options with a recommendation.

## 3. Idea starters

- **Benefit of the benefit.** People buy the hole, not the drill bit (Sullivan p.41).
- **Incontestable fact.** If there's a checkable fact (it lasts 20 years), prefer it to puffery (Sullivan pp.41-42).
- **The weakness inside the competitor's strength** (Avis vs. Hertz). Try the rival's product and note what's genuinely good about it (Sullivan p.41).
- **The naive question** insiders skip. DDB's VW snowplow spot came from asking how the snowplow driver gets to his snowplow (Sullivan p.39).
- **Your own money.** What would make you buy it? What would you do if you were paying for the campaign? (Sullivan pp.39-40)
- **Turn the negative into the proof** (VW "Lemon", Guinness's slow pour, Marmite).
- **Go 180** - do the opposite of the category, but for a reason that comes from the product; provocation for its own sake fails (Sullivan pp.51-52, 57-58, citing Bernbach).
- **The inverse.** What doesn't the product do? Who doesn't need it? (Sullivan p.58)
- **Show, don't announce.** Saying "we're cool" isn't being cool; find a demonstration (Sullivan pp.56-57).
- **Exaggerate a true benefit** - but distrust it as a first move: exaggeration is where everyone lands first (Sullivan pp.59-60).
- **Stretch the product** - bigger, smaller, upside down, used the wrong way, standing in for something else (Sullivan pp.54-55).
- **Take the product away** (Got Milk?).
- **Fake quote / implication** - let the reader finish the joke (The Economist).
- **Interrogate your own slogan** to refresh it (Nike "Why Do It?", 2025).
- **Run mental operations** on the problem: reverse, shrink, combine, substitute, invert, understate. Or imagine how a particular brand, agency or filmmaker would solve it (Sullivan pp.60-61).
- **Word collisions.** List words from the product's world, pair two at random, see what the collision suggests (Sullivan pp.45-46). For names and titles, also pool sensory words (see, feel, sound, reminds me of) and combine across columns (Futur Visual Vocabulary).
- **Customer words first.** Interview answers to "describe us in five words" and "what did we actually do for you?" are the first source for customer-language lines (SCADpro discovery questions).
- **Change the product instead of advertising a claim** (a second beer label with conversation starters proved sociability rather than claiming it; Sullivan pp.188-189).
- Avoid the "say one thing, show another" pun unless the gap is startling (Sullivan p.58).

## 4. Generating in volume

- **Two passes.** First a fast, unfiltered dump (Sullivan suggests about 20 minutes). Then a slow pass aiming at 100 lines that are all decent or better (p.89).
- **Count doors, not lines.** Break the job into ways in - one attribute, benefit, place, history or use at a time - and write a batch for each; each door opens more doors (pp.84-89).
- **Cover the wall.** The brain wants closure early; resist signing off on the first passable idea. Sullivan says the great one often arrives around the 500th (pp.81-82).
- **Mark favourites, move them to a clean page, start again** as if you had nothing (p.89).
- **Alternate generator and critic.** Let the loose generator go first, then switch to a cold critic who checks against the strategy (pp.58-59).
- **Permission to be bad.** A rough draft is a workbench (pp.43-44).
- **When stuck:** switch format (write the body if the headline won't come), get original research, browse the product's physical world, go to the shelf where it's sold, dig many shallow holes instead of one deep one (pp.106-110).
- **Know when to stop generating** and spend the remaining time making the good ones great (p.79).
- **When collaborating with a user, never dismiss a weak idea.** Reshape it and hand it back; look for what's right in it first (p.44).

## 5. Judging ideas

- **Dru's two questions** (Sullivan pp.32, 43): could this have been made without the brief? (then it lacks content). Is it just the brief transcribed? (then it lacks a creative leap).
- **The overlap test** (pp.37-38): it must be on strategy *and* distinctive. Dull fails one way, irrelevant brilliance the other.
- **The interesting part must be the sales message** (pp.66-68). If the hook could be cut away from the product point without loss, it's decoration. If the concept bores you, you haven't found the interesting thing in the product yet - go back to the product.
- **Doesn't look or sound like an ad** (pp.66-67).
- **Signs of a big idea** (p.79): almost insultingly simple, has legs across media, may drift off the written strategy. If it's clearly better, retool the strategy rather than kill the idea.
- **"We can't do that, can we?"** - laughing out loud and doubting you could run it is a good sign; the block is usually an imagined client (pp.83, 183).
- **Would anyone talk about it?** If nobody would mention it, it may lack tension (p.183).
- **Novelty is hard for the maker to judge.** Genuinely new work feels unrelated to anything, so you distrust it. Don't discard an odd option just because it's uncomfortable; compare it head to head and test it for relevance - the one rule that always holds (pp.199-200).
- **Trott's three:** impact, communication, persuasion.
- **Checklist:** true and believable · relevant · one point, fast · shows rather than tells · has a villain or tension · retellable in a sentence · only this brand could run it (or, in a parity category, we said it first) · builds an asset the brand can own for years.

Sullivan's own summary of a big idea (p.194): believable, relevant, simple, doesn't look like an ad, opens strong, one theme, shows rather than tells, works fast, few moving parts, has a villain, tells the truth, simple language, entertaining throughout, leaves a picture in the mind, ends strong, and good enough that people share it unpaid.

## 6. Campaigns and platforms

- **Map a day in the customer's life and the buying path** (awareness, shortlist, comparison, store, trial). List contact points, pick a small interesting one, and think big from there (Sullivan pp.176-178).
- **Pretend TV and print don't exist.** How would you sell it then? (p.178)
- **A platform is a one-line idea rebuilt natively for each medium,** with one look and one message; each piece must stand alone (pp.180-182). Test an idea by stretching it into other formats (sticker, cup, web page) to see if it has room (pp.278-281).
- **A set of options or a campaign is one idea in several forms,** not several one-offs sharing a typeface (pp.278-281).
- **Kill the weak sister.** Readers meet executions one at a time, so replace any that is merely okay (p.106).
- **Ask what business idea sits behind the line** - strong ideas shape a product, service or position, not just an ad (pp.273-275).
- **Platforms run for years.** Field's IPA data: creativity pays when deployed long-term and consistently.

## 7. Social-era updates (later editions and current practice)

- Think in loops: the brand **does** something, **invites** participation, **captures** it, **shares** it.
- Move while the moment is live.
- Make the idea bigger, not the logo (also argued in the 3rd ed. pp.262-264).
- Online, only information or entertainment earn attention; entertainment spreads because sharing says something about the sender (3rd ed. pp.192-195).
- Viral can't be decided in advance: make something extremely good, then seed it.
- Out-of-home and guerrilla placements should delight or inform, never accost; people can't switch them off (pp.189-191).
