## 1. Manifestos
Structure that recurs in the famous ones:
1. **Name the tribe or the enemy** - a type of person or a belief, not the product.
2. **Build with rhythm** - short parallel lines, repeated openings.
3. **Turn** - one line flips the expected meaning.
4. **Land the belief** - one statement the brand can own.
5. **Brand last, if at all.**

| Example | Move to steal |
|---|---|
| Apple "Here's to the crazy ones" (1997) | Take the audience's stigma and make it a badge; never mention the product |
| Nike "Why Do It?" (2025) | Voice the audience's doubts, then answer with the line |
| Holstee Manifesto (2009) | Make it hangable - it works with no logo |
| Lululemon bag manifesto | A manifesto can be a list of tenets |
| Patagonia "Don't Buy This Jacket" (2011) | Contradict the commercial expectation to prove the belief |

Rules: under 150 words for film, under 250 for print. No statistics: turn the fact into a scene or a human line ("Most of us started after thirty", not "60% of members"). Don't open by restating the audience's objection in the brand's voice ("X is for the young. That's what you've heard.") - it's the most predictable opening; start inside the reader's life instead. Cut every line a competitor could sign. If it has no rhythm read aloud, it isn't a manifesto. Avoid generic uplift ("dream big", "unleash").

## 2. Scripts
Format: two columns (VIDEO | AUDIO) for agency TV; mark SFX, MUSIC, VO, SUPER.

| Length | Job | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| 6s | One idea, one image | Visual hook > twist > logo; no VO exposition |
| 15s | One scene or joke | About 10 usable seconds once the logo and wrap-up take theirs (Sullivan p.127). One locked-off scene; even two cuts look choppy. Strip it twice. |
| 30s | A story with one turn | Situation > complication > product resolves > line |
| 60s | Character arc or manifesto | Character and place > escalation > peak > line |
| TikTok / Reels | Earn 1-2 seconds | Open on action, a face or dialogue; never a logo; captions |

Judge a script: who is in it, what happens, where? Dialogue rather than VO? Is the brand essential to the story? Does the ending reward a second view?

## 3. Posters / OOH
- One thought per poster. The punchline sits where the eye ends. Brand legible at distance.
- Word-count rules are guidelines. Long-copy posters work where people wait (platforms, stations) if line one earns the rest.
- The Economist: one witty line, white on red, no picture - the logo is the layout.
- Surreal: setup headline, deflating small print, built to be photographed and shared. Caution: in one consumer test most people missed the joke and believed the celebrity claim, and the ads said little about the product.
- Write for the photo - posters are now social content.

## 4. Radio / audio
- Write for the ear: short sentences, one idea, brand name early and at least twice.
- Sound effects and voices create place and character cheaply.
- About 60 words for a spare 30-second spot (Sullivan p.156); 75-80 only if the idea depends on speed. Write and time the legal tags first, then write into what is left. Read it slowly with a stopwatch.
- Be interesting within five seconds: the listener is doing something else.
- Decide what the listener should feel before picking a tone. Humour is everyone's default on radio, so a straight, single-voice spot often stands out.
- Build the sale into the premise. Avoid the "gag, then serious announcer, then gag" shape.
- Dialogue must sound like real speech (fragments, interruptions). Never make characters say the sales message to each other; give that to a straight voice.
- Ban-list (Sullivan pp.153-154): fake game shows, call-ins and newscasts; "I'm here at..." openings; answering-machine bits; cartoon voices; parody jingles; jingles generally.
- With a sonic logo, speak the brand name before it and keep the sonic logo at the very end (Hyundai's rule).

## 5. Creative brief (one page)
1. Why are we advertising? (business problem)
2. Who are we talking to? (a person)
3. What do they think, feel, do now?
4. What do we want them to think, feel, do?
5. **Insight:** a human truth with tension.
6. **Single-minded proposition:** one sentence, no "and".
7. Reasons to believe.
8. Voice and tone.
9. Mandatories.

| Bad insight (a fact) | Good insight (a truth with tension) |
|---|---|
| "Millennials care about sustainability." | "People want to buy less but feel guilty throwing out things that still work." |
| "Milk is healthy." | "You only notice milk when it's gone." (Got Milk?) |
| "Customers want fast banking." | "Checking your balance feels like stepping on the scales after Christmas." |

Test: a good insight makes the listener say "that's so true" and suggests a tension the brand can resolve.

## 6. Positioning and platforms
- Geoffrey Moore template (internal, never published as copy): For [target] who [need], [product] is a [category] that [benefit]. Unlike [alternative], we [differentiator].
- Platform stack: belief > positioning > personality traits > voice principles > messaging pillars > tagline. Copy should trace back to it.
- For B2B, prefer Dunford's sequence (06-conversion-copy.md).

## 7. Naming
- Types: descriptive, suggestive, invented, founder, metaphor.
- Screen: pronounceable, spellable from hearing, domain and handle, trademark conflicts, meanings in other languages.
- Generate 200+, cut to 20, test 5 in sentences ("Have you tried ___?").

## 8. Script and poster craft (Sullivan, 3rd ed.)

**TV and video**
- **Write the last five seconds first.** The close resolves the rest; if you can't write it, you don't have a premise yet (pp.125-126).
- **Open with something visually odd in the first two seconds** to create a curiosity gap (p.125).
- **Sound-off test:** could a viewer follow it with the volume down? Not mandatory, but a sign of a simple, visual idea (pp.120-121).
- **Find one great image, then build a beginning, middle and end around it.** A spot with one moving part is a print ad wasting the medium (pp.121-122).
- **Audio and video complete each other;** they never repeat each other. An understated voice over a dramatic image creates tension (p.128).
- **Entertain to the last frame.** No long build to a gimmick twist; a known switcheroo has no replay value (p.126).
- **Write sparely** so moments can breathe without voiceover over everything (p.127).
- **Simple wins at any budget:** small budgets force the essence; big ones put the money on screen (pp.118-125).
- **Long-form response video:** self-contained 8-10 minute segments, each with its own call to action, since half the audience leaves before 20 minutes (pp.143-146).

**Posters and outdoor**
- Aim for three words; accept up to about seven plus the logo (pp.74-76).
- **Two-second flip test:** show the layout for two seconds and take it away. Did they get it?
- Outdoor is where to be bold and give passers-by something back; they can't turn the page (pp.77-78).
- **Postcard vs. letter:** a visual-led piece with a sliver of copy suits a simple point; a copy-led piece suits a complex one, where the weight of copy itself signals substance (pp.46-47).
- For scan-heavy formats, assume nobody reads body copy: the selling idea lives in the headline or visual (pp.52-54).

**Manifestos: a rallying-phrase page** (LA Chargers guide) can run: what the phrase means beyond its surface, what it asks people to do, the brand's qualities, a history line, a nod to doubters, a light invitation. Avoid its opening move ("more than a chant") - see 14.

## 9. Intake and customer interviews

**Intake questions** (Futur creative brief questionnaire): What is the one thing this must do? Where will it live, in what format? Who is it for, what do they care about, what must they see? How should they feel afterwards? What must be kept from what exists? What must we always and never do? Which examples do you like, and why?

**A production brief** beside the creative brief, for projects with real logistics: title, overview, goal, success metrics, deliverables and specs, stakeholders, persona(s), key messages, tone, budget, timeline, *related current projects* (the field most briefs miss), extra references (Column Five).

**First client conversation, four moves** (Futur first-meeting worksheet): connect and ask broad business questions; understand what they do and how they measure success (talk little); align on outcomes, timeline, decision-makers and budget; read back a summary and agree next steps. Useful probes: how did your last creative partnership go? If time and money were no object, what would the ideal result be? Who decides, and what influences them?

**Customer interviews** (SCADpro B2B discovery questions): group questions by role and reporting line, this year's objectives and how they're judged, top challenges, current alternatives, who else decides, budget path, who they turn to, where they read, how they judge return, and how working with the client felt. Technique: after the first answer to "why did you buy?", ask "anything else?" twice; ask them to describe the offer in five words or fewer; ask what the work ultimately did for them and listen for verbs like sped up, replaced, freed up, turned around. Those answers are raw headlines.

**Positioning add-ons** (Column Five brand strategy toolkit):
- Differentiation fill-in: "Our [offering] is the only [category] that [benefit]." If a rival could sign it, the benefit isn't specific enough.
- Messaging architecture: one tagline, one value proposition, three pillars, up to three proof points each. Every piece traces to one pillar.
- Brand heart as four separate answers: purpose (why we exist), vision (the future we help create), mission (what we do and how), values (3-5 short verb-led phrases, each with one sentence on what it means in practice). Missions that name a concrete standard or act read as more ownable than world-scale slogans.
- Personas: name, channels, goals, pain points - and a final row for the message to carry to that persona. Cap at three. A persona without a message row changes nothing.
- Campaign sheet: concept, why it works, one-line campaign tagline, internal primary message (never published), supporting benefits, talking points, formats, channels, sample copy.
- Content series statement: "We will [action] by [how or where] so that [goal]." Reject a series that can't fill the last blank.
- Personal-branding sentence as a quick check: I help [one community] by [up to three things] so that [outcome] and [feeling]. Watch for abstract endings that fail the competitor swap.

**Naming add-ons:** a name with a short true story does the memory work - philosophy-as-name (an agency called Blind because it starts with no preconceptions), an origin object (a founder's stone behind a logo), family name plus descriptor. Explain a storied name in about three sentences up front.

## 10. Capabilities decks, proposals, About pages

- **Section titles as the reader's next question,** or a line a person would say ("So what do you actually do?", "Prove it", "What do clients say?") - the deck reads as a conversation.
- **Name the method and gloss each step with a plain question** (belief: what do you believe? purpose: what will you do about it? behaviour: how do you live it?).
- **Frame each service as the client's question,** with short output nouns beneath; scannable columns beat prose.
- **One authority line, then pivot to the reader,** can justify a method without argument.
- **Proposals:** scope every phase with counts and limits (concepts, rounds, variants, support hours, fees on pass-through work). Counts prevent disputes and show maturity.
- **Portfolio openers:** a first-person statement with a credential count tied to the reader's place or need. Relevance before biography.
- **Anchor each rationale to a physical fact** (a facade date, a waterfront, a ferry route); drop the stock luxury adjectives.
- **Keep one grammatical person** through a proposal; mixed I/we/they reads as pasted together.
- **About Us:** positioning line, checkable proof of craft (visitors can watch through glass walls; the tasting-room tables are made in-house), then an invitation.
- **Thought-leadership pieces:** coin a two-word concept, define it in under 40 words, build the structure from its parts, quote named experts for claims, and close by returning to the opening problems one segment at a time.
- **Redefine a loaded word in the brand's own terms** and set it large; one owned definition is more quotable than ten claims.
- **Before presenting any concept, answer** (Futur pitch evaluation): the concept in one sentence; how it serves the business goal; the message and why the audience cares; what you offer that rivals don't; your proof; how the client will judge; which objections are handled.

## 11. Pitch emails and content plans

- **Media and influencer pitches:** pitch a story, not the brand - why it matters to their readers or fits a story they already cover. Read a dozen of their pieces first. For influencers, a soft pitch of a few bullets asking if they'd like more. Offer a short exclusive. Build the relationship before you need coverage (content distribution guide).
- **Long-form plans:** when writing a core piece (white paper, e-book, long post), mark the stats, quotes and sections that will become short pieces. Plan three buckets: core, divisible, timely.

## 12. Payment and follow-up emails (Paid in Full, Ben Burns)

- **Tone:** keeping an agreement, not begging. Calm, courteous, clear; assume the best at first and firm up only as the due date passes.
- **Ladder, scheduled in advance:** a receipt check a couple of days after sending; a friendly reminder about a week before due; a short due-date note; an overdue notice about a month later citing the agreed late fee; a thank-you after payment. Each a little more direct, none accusatory, each shorter than the last and readable on its own.
- **Subject lines as signposts:** which invoice (e.g. "1 of 3"), what it's for, the due date. Later reminders can reply in the same thread.
- **Body:** a warm opener, one plain sentence with invoice number, amount and due date, the link or attachment, any early-payment incentive, then close on a specific question ("When will this be processed?") - a reply proves it arrived.
- **Address the person who pays,** copy the person who owns the relationship, and send from a billing address so it reads as routine process.
- **Tie deadlines to the project** ("so we can start on time"), not to your cash flow. Mention early-payment benefits early and late fees only on the due-date and overdue notes.
- **Always thank after payment,** attach the receipt, and at project end ask what you can help with next.
- **When a client goes silent,** widen the circle before escalating and check on the person; there may be a good reason.
- The same ladder fits sales follow-ups, grant-report reminders and vendor or donor nudges.
