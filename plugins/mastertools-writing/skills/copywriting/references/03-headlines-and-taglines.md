# Headlines and taglines

Contents: 1. Headline rules · 2. Match to awareness and sophistication · 3. Headline types · 4. Tagline construction · 5. Tests · 6. Craft notes from Hey Whipple and brand guides

## 1. Headline rules

- **Do the selling in the headline.** Far more people read the headline than the body (Ogilvy).
- **Say the point plainly.** No "blind" headlines that only make sense after the body.
- **Promise a benefit or deliver news.**
- **Make line one pull the reader to line two** (Sugarman's slippery slide; Harry Dry's "open a gap").
- **Visual, falsifiable, ownable** (Harry Dry): can I picture it, could it be proven false, could nobody else say it?
- **Lead with the unique fact.** "Write the title only you can."

## 2. Match the reader (Schwartz)

| Reader awareness | Headline job |
|---|---|
| Unaware | Story, identity or curiosity hook; don't lead with the product |
| Problem-aware | Name the problem in their words |
| Solution-aware | Show your mechanism or why your way is better |
| Product-aware | Proof, comparison, offer |
| Most aware | The deal |

| Market sophistication | Claim strategy |
|---|---|
| New category | State the claim plainly |
| Competitors arriving | Bigger claim + proof |
| Noisy | A new mechanism |
| Mechanism copied | Better / easier version of it |
| Saturated | Sell identity and belonging |

## 3. Headline types

| Type | Pattern | Example | Why it works |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct benefit | Outcome, not category | "Acing the SAT." (rewrite of "The next generation of SAT prep") | The kid wants the outcome, not a product class |
| Specific fact | One surprising detail | Ogilvy's Rolls-Royce electric clock | One fact proves the whole quality claim |
| Story | Setback, then reversal | Caples: "They laughed when I sat down at the piano..." | Humiliation and triumph in one line |
| Question | Implicates the reader | "Do you make these mistakes in English?" | Readers check themselves |
| Confession | Admit a flaw, flip it | Avis: only No. 2, so we try harder | The confession makes the promise believable |
| One word | Shock, then reveal | VW "Lemon." | An insult that turns into proof of rigour |
| Fake quote | A character voices it | Economist "I never read The Economist." - Management trainee. Aged 42. | The reader supplies the punchline |
| Customer words | Lift a verbatim | "If you think you need rehab, you do." | Mirrors the reader's private thought |
| Reverse | Tell them not to | Patagonia "Don't Buy This Jacket" | Breaks the script, proves the values |
| Contrast | Before vs. after in numbers | "Close the books in 3 days, not 10." | The number shows the gain |

House style: the Question and Customer-words rows (and Caples' story line) use "you" or "I". Keep the move, drop the address: "Do you make these mistakes in English?" becomes "The five English mistakes that give people away."

## 4. Tagline construction

1. **Short.** Most classics run two to five words.
2. **Rhythm.** Monosyllables, parallelism ("Have a break, have a Kit Kat"), rhyme ("Wow no cow"), triads ("work, rest and play").
3. **Own a behaviour or stance, not an adjective.** "We try harder" is a behaviour; "quality service" is wallpaper.
4. **Double meanings only when both readings help** ("When it rains it pours").
5. **Break grammar on purpose** when the break acts out the message ("Think Different").
6. **Make it a platform.** It should suggest twenty executions (Got Milk?, Priceless).
7. **Hard to copy, and tied to the product.** If a competitor can swap in their name, rewrite. Then run the category swap: if the line would work just as well for an umbrella, a bank or a car, it isn't about this product. The strongest taglines fuse the product's act (eat, drive, wear, bank) with what the brand owns ("Melts in your mouth, not in your hand"; "The Ultimate Driving Machine"). A clever idiom that never touches the product loses to a plainer line that does.
8. **Built to run for years.** Repetition is how a line becomes an asset. Refresh it rather than replace it.
9. **Check for bad readings and bad origins.**

## 5. Tests

- Works without the logo and said aloud?
- True of this brand, odd for competitors?
- Suggests twenty executions?
- Would the brand still say it in ten years?
- Volume rule (Sullivan, 3rd ed. p.89): when a piece needs a headline, write 100 - not a fast dump but 100 lines that are all decent or better, written slowly. Do the fast first-thought pass separately, before it. Count "doors" too: write a batch per angle (origin, use, history, place), since the goal is many ways of seeing the problem, not many wordings of one.
- Poster word-count "rules" (e.g. seven words) are guidelines, not laws.

## 6. Craft notes from *Hey Whipple* (3rd ed.) and brand guides

- **Put the reveal word at the end** of the headline. A reveal mid-sentence loses its surprise (Sullivan pp.91-92).
- **Puns: write them down, then throw them out.** They have no persuasive value (p.84). Many mainstream brand guides ban them outright (Hyundai).
- **No invented named characters** ("little Timmy...") - readers spot fake names and resent them (p.92).
- **No model numbers or product codes in headlines** - numerals that aren't the point are speed bumps (p.95).
- **Word and picture divide the labour.** A bold visual gets a plain headline; a headline that does the work gets a quiet visual. Never show what you say or say what you show (pp.92-94).
- **Detail beats assertion.** Three concrete facts will hold a reader that one claim won't (p.103).
- **Worn openers** ("Contrary to popular belief...", "Something is wrong when...") - if you think you've seen it, drop it (pp.94-95).
- **Conflict in a headline is fine** as long as the net takeaway is positive; tension makes a message dramatic (pp.254-256).
- **Ask whether you need a tagline at all.** It's one more element competing for attention; most add no information. Write it early, as an anthem bigger than the product, or skip it (pp.104-105).
- **The slogan word-grid test.** If your tagline could be assembled from commitment / excellence / tomorrow / quality / innovation / solutions, it's interchangeable - start again (pp.104-105).
- **Read every tagline in its worst possible tone** to catch an unintended second meaning (p.105).
- **Deliver taglines with usage notes:** where it runs, what it may sit beside, and that it is never reworded or stacked with a campaign line (Pebble style guide template).
- **Offer a tagline family** when a brand has several product lines or rituals: one primary line, secondary lines per theme.
- **Respect a brand's word caps** as real mandatories (Hyundai: around five words for headlines, product names inside a full sentence rather than as a bare label).
- **Test a modern platform line in messaging contexts** - as a greeting, a reply, a caption, with an emoji (LA Chargers' rallying phrase).
