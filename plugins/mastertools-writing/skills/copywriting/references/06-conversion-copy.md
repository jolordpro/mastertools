## 1. Research first
- **Voice of customer.** Mine reviews (yours and competitors'), surveys, sales calls, support tickets, forums. Sort phrases into before-state pains, dream outcomes, objections. Lift the best phrasing verbatim (Wiebe).
- **Position before you write** (Dunford): competitive alternatives (including "do nothing") > unique attributes > value and proof > best-fit customer > category.
- **Diagnose awareness** (Schwartz; see 03) to choose where the page starts.
- If none of this exists, say what you assumed and mark customer-language slots as placeholders.

## 2. Landing page skeleton (medium freedom)
1. **Headline:** bold, specific outcome for the best-fit customer.
2. **Subhead:** how it works or who it's for, in one plain sentence.
3. **Primary CTA** + the product doing the job.
4. **Social proof:** numbers, logos, a named testimonial in the customer's words.
5. **Problem / before state** in customer language.
6. **How it works:** three steps.
7. **Benefits backed by features**, each visual and checkable.
8. **Objections:** price, switching cost, risk, "will it work for me?"
9. **Proof round two:** case study, data, guarantee.
10. **Final CTA** restating the outcome.

Bold claim at the top, then a ridiculous amount of proof (Harry Dry).

## 3. Value props and CTAs
- Headline = outcome. Subhead = mechanism + audience.
- Five-second test: can a stranger say what you sell, to whom, and why it's better?
- Clarity above the fold; save wordplay for brand moments.
- Name the actual thing - never "solution", "platform", "seamless".
- **CTAs:** name the value, not the action ("Get the free audit", "See the outfits", "Start the free trial") - no "my" or "your" (house style; first-person buttons have tested well elsewhere, e.g. Dressipi in 12). Put risk reducers next to the button ("No card needed", "Cancel anytime"). Never "Submit".

## 4. Frameworks: when to use

| Framework | Use when | Watch out |
|---|---|---|
| PAS (problem, agitate, solve) | Known, painful problem; short emails and ads | Over-agitation feels manipulative |
| AIDA | Long sales pages, cold traffic | Too generic to guide line choices |
| BAB (before, after, bridge) | Transformational products; social | The "after" must be specific and credible |
| 4 Us (useful, urgent, unique, ultra-specific) | Scoring headlines and subject lines | Fake urgency erodes trust |
| Rule of One (one reader, idea, promise, offer) | Any landing page or email | Stakeholders want everything |
| JTBD / switch interviews (Moesta) | Researching why people switch: push, pull, anxiety, habit | A research method, not a template |
| StoryBrand (customer as hero, brand as guide) | Fixing muddled websites fast | Produces lookalike sites; weak for brand work |

## 5. Email
- Permission first (Godin): anticipated, personal, relevant.
- Subject line: specific, curious or useful; written like a person. Preview text adds something new.
- One idea, one CTA. Open with the reader, never "We're excited to announce". Short paragraphs. Sign off as a human.
- When the email answers a long-standing request or a pain the company caused, own it in a plain, human line ("Three years of requests. Finally here.") rather than explaining the company's reasons.
- Subject-line statistics online are mostly anecdotal; test.

## 6. Social
- **Short video:** earn the first 1-2 seconds - open on action, a face or a line of dialogue, never a logo. Native creator grammar. Captions for sound-off.
- **X / Threads:** reply culture, speed and wit; brevity is native.
- **Everywhere:** comment like a person; be useful or entertaining, ideally both.

## 7. Microcopy rules (low freedom: follow exactly)
- **Buttons:** verb + object ("Add product", "Send gallery"). Test: the label should complete the thought "I want to..." without itself containing "I", "my" or "your".
- **Confirmation dialogs:** buttons state the outcome ("Delete file" / "Keep file"), never Yes / No.
- **Errors:** what happened + how to fix it. No blame, no codes, no "invalid".
- **Empty states:** why it's empty + one next action.
- **Forms:** labels above fields; show the format; explain why you need sensitive data.
- **Dialog titles:** five words or fewer ("Delete this gallery?"). Put the stake in the body, not the title.
- **Empty-state buttons:** make the first action feel small and personal ("Send the first gallery").
- **Contractions:** use them in friendly product copy ("hasn't", "won't") - they sound human. Avoid negative contractions only in high-stakes legal or government instructions, where readers can misread them (GOV.UK).
- **Apologies:** say sorry once, plainly, then say what you're doing (Monzo).
- Sentence case. No exclamation marks in errors. Name the specific thing (the file, the client) with placeholders like [FILE NAME] when the UI can fill them.

## 8. Evidence (with strength)

| Finding | Source | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Copy at grade 5-7 converted at 11.1% median vs 5.3% for professional-level | Unbounce 2024, 41,000 pages | Robust correlation, not causal; reverses in legal |
| Median landing-page conversion 6.6%; SaaS lowest at 3.8% | Unbounce 2024 | Robust benchmark |

## 9. Brand voice + conversion
- Voice is the constant; conversion structure is the skeleton. Fill the skeleton in the voice.
- Taglines build memory over time; page headlines win the click now. Don't force the tagline into the H1 unless it also states the value.
- Carry distinctive assets (phrases, characters, colours) onto every conversion surface.

## 10. Mission-driven copy (secondary)
- One specific person or outcome beats statistics.
- Name the tribe ("people like us give to causes like this").
- Prove values with behaviour.
- Make the ask concrete (what $X does) and the CTA immediate.
- Sharpen the enemy, not the guilt.

## 11. Body copy (Sullivan, 3rd ed. pp.98-106)
- Few people read body copy, but the few who do are your best prospects. Write it well and stop when you've said it.
- **No pre-ramble.** The first paragraph must not restate the headline; open with the most interesting or persuasive point you have.
- **Know the argument's route before you write** (off the headline, to A, to B, land on C), then link sentences so none could be lifted out.
- **Write to one visualised person, as if in a letter.**
- **Many short paragraphs.** Long blocks look like work.
- **End on the practical next step,** not a snappy last line.
- **Cut by a third,** read aloud, proofread by eye, then show two colleagues: if several flag the same thing, it's real.

## 12. Response copy (Sullivan pp.134-146)
- **Brand copy and response copy are different jobs:** emotion builds the brand; reason and a clear next step drive response.
- **Write the call to action first.** It forces you to boil the offer down.
- **Hold the phone number or link until the offer is explained.** Showing it from the first second brings in people who then reject the offer.
- **More information sells more in response copy - if it's organised** inside a story or conversation, with the strongest points only.
- **Alternate rational support with the emotional payoff** every two or three benefits.
- Be clear before clever; say the product's name plainly; mention a free offer twice without shouting. Stock "magic words" (free, new, revolutionary) are what weak products lean on.
- No fake formats (fake talk show, fake news, fake call-in).

## 13. Journey stages and content (Column Five toolkits; content distribution guide)
- Map copy to five stages - awareness, consideration, analysis, purchase, loyalty - and for each write what the reader needs to hear, feel and do next. Loyalty (onboarding, renewal, retention) is a separate job most copy forgets.
- Critique content per piece: which stage, which persona, does it sound like the brand, is there one clear CTA, how is it promoted.
- Shareable editorial headlines: lead with or include a verb; roughly 60-100 characters for blog and social posts (ad headlines can be much shorter).
- Email: send from a named person, not a generic address; segment so copy can reference what the reader did; no symbols in subject lines.
- Every long post ends with exactly one next action matched to the reader's stage.
- Web layout: body lines around 45-70 characters, left-aligned, avoid one-word last lines.

## 14. Case studies, testimonials and bios (capabilities decks in the reference library)
- **Case study shape:** client, deliverables, challenge (with a tension inside it - grow sales *without* alienating existing fans), outcome, a strip of labelled numbers, then phases in short labelled paragraphs. End the outcome on a human word after the data.
- **Short case blurbs** (three or four sentences) lead with the from-to move: what the brand was, what it became. Offer the full study on request.
- **Testimonials:** one per slide or block, with name, title and company. Prefer ones describing a behaviour ("solved problems fast") over pure praise.
- **Team bios** list credentials plus one human detail, and can end by turning to what the person can do for the reader.
