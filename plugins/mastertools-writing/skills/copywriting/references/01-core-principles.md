# Core principles

Contents: A. What every school agrees on · B. Where experts disagree and how to decide · C. The 15 rules

## A. Agreed principles (state of the canon)

| # | Rule | Why | Lineage |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Make one point. | Every extra point weakens the main one. | Sullivan; Trott's single-minded proposition |
| 2 | Tell the truth, especially the inconvenient one. | Honesty is rare, so it stands out; a confessed flaw becomes proof. | Bernbach/DDB (VW, Avis); Sullivan; Patagonia |
| 3 | Be specific. | Concrete claims get pictured, believed, remembered. | Hopkins; Ogilvy; Harry Dry |
| 4 | Sell the benefit, prove it with the feature. | People buy outcomes and feelings; features are evidence. | Ogilvy; Hopkins; Godin |
| 5 | Respect the reader's intelligence. | Readers know they are being sold to. | Ogilvy |
| 6 | Use the reader's language. | Familiar words are processed faster and trusted more. | Ogilvy; Wiebe; Mailchimp |
| 7 | Earn attention; never assume it. | Most advertising is not noticed at all. | Trott (impact > communication > persuasion); Godin (Purple Cow) |
| 8 | Find the tension. | Drama is conflict: an enemy, a convention, a contradiction. | Sullivan; Liquid Death |
| 9 | Be simple. | Simple ideas survive retelling. | Sullivan; Ogilvy; Ritson |
| 10 | Cut. | Every spare word weakens its neighbours. | Harry Dry; Sullivan |
| 11 | Surprise. | The expected is invisible. | Bernbach; Sutherland ("the opposite of a good idea can also be a good idea") |
| 12 | Sound human. | Conversation persuades; speeches lecture. | Sullivan; Mailchimp; Godin |
| 13 | Be consistent and distinctive. | Brands grow by being easy to recall and recognise. | Sharp/Romaniuk; Ritson |
| 14 | Know exactly who it is for. | A person beats a demographic. | Godin (smallest viable audience); Dunford |
| 15 | Know which job the copy does. | Brand copy and activation copy follow different rules. | Binet & Field (see 10-effectiveness-evidence.md) |
| 16 | Character, incident, place. | If you cannot say who, what happens and where, the idea is probably abstract. | Orlando Wood / System1 |
| 17 | Specificity is the antidote to sameness. | Generic copy - human or AI - converges on the average. | Doshi & Hauser 2024; Harry Dry |

## B. Disagreements and decision rules

| Debate | Rule |
|---|---|
| Long vs. short | Length follows the reader's awareness and the size of the decision. Short for unaware readers, impulse buys, outdoor. Long for considered purchases and product-aware readers. Never pad. |
| Clever vs. clear | Clarity is required; cleverness is a bonus. A clever line must still land in one read. Brand work can carry more wit; conversion pages lead with clarity. |
| Brand vs. performance | Use both. Voice sets how you sound; the page's job sets what you say. Test headlines, keep the voice. |
| Differentiation vs. distinctiveness | Write lines that are both recognisably ours (voice, assets) and claim more of something buyers care about. |
| Purpose vs. product | Use purpose only when the brand's behaviour proves it and the audience cares. Otherwise sell the product. |

## C. The 15 rules (memorise)

1. Say one thing.
2. Start from the truth, unflattering parts included.
3. Be specific enough to picture and to check.
4. Write what only this brand could say.
5. Lead with the reader's self-interest.
6. Find the tension.
7. Write in volume before choosing.
8. Get noticed first.
9. Clarity before cleverness on conversion surfaces.
10. Steal the customer's words.
11. Write like one person talking to one person.
12. Keep the voice; move the tone with the reader's mood.
13. Pick a small, specific audience.
14. Cut hard, then read it aloud.
15. Make the brand unmistakable and stay consistent for years.

## D. From *Hey Whipple* (3rd ed.) and the reference library

- **Reduce the brand to one word.** People file brands under a single adjective (Volvo: safe). Write every piece to reinforce it; secondary benefits belong on deeper pages (Sullivan pp.26-29).
- **If the good adjectives are taken, flank.** Don't settle for the second-best synonym; take the opposite or coin a new one (Heinz owned "slow" while rivals fought over who tasted most of tomatoes, Sullivan p.28).
- **Simple is almost always better.** Sullivan's one takeaway from the whole book (p.68): simple ideas are bigger, easier to remember and the only thing that cuts through clutter.
- **Leave part of the work to the reader.** An idea the reader completes in their own head sticks. Calibrate: leave too little out and it bores, too much and it baffles (Sullivan pp.61-65).
- **Interesting first; funny is only one kind of interesting** (pp.65-66).
- **Write like you talk.** Sullivan's demonstration: a 143-word corporate memo that meant nine words. One-word sentences, sentences starting with "and", ending on a preposition - all fine if it sounds natural and is clear (pp.99-100).
- **Ownership can come from execution.** In parity categories, a claim a rival could make is still yours if your voice, concept and tone got there first. Aim for "a competitor couldn't sign it"; accept "we said it first" when the facts are shared (pp.256-257).
- **Empty is the default failure.** When there's nothing specific to say ("we care", "people first"), don't dress it up; push for one real story or fact (pp.214-216). This matters most for service providers that can't claim to be better than competitors.
- **Capture a felt moment.** When the brief names a feeling (first-home nerves, the job interview), build the piece around that feeling played straight, not around a device (pp.200-203).
- **Most buying is emotional, with reasons added after** (pp.203, 244) - practitioner belief, consistent with the IPA evidence in 10.
